Button Styles and Interactions

View the Live Design System

Explore the interactive EcoPackage button design system in action

Last month, I watched a client spend $15,000 on a beautiful website redesign. Three weeks after launch, their conversion rate actually dropped. The culprit? Their buttons were gorgeous—but users couldn't figure out what to click.

That moment reminded me why I love button design. It's not about making pretty rectangles. It's about the split second when a user hovers over an element, unsure if it's clickable, wondering if this is the right choice. That split second? That's where we either earn their trust or lose their business.

Let me tell you about a design system I recently worked with that got it right.

The Eco-Brand That Understood Hierarchy

The project was for EcoPackage, a sustainable food packaging company. They came to me with a problem many brands face: "We want our buttons to reflect our eco-friendly values, but we also need them to actually work."

Their initial mockups had six different button colors. Green for eco-actions, brown for purchases, sage for information, and on it went. Beautiful? Sure. Usable? Not even close. Users in testing kept pausing, scanning, trying to decode which button meant what. Every pause is a moment of friction. Every moment of friction is a potential bounce.

We started over with one color: Earthy Brown (#8b6f47). Just one.

"But won't that be boring?" they asked.

Here's what I told them: "Think about traffic lights. Red, yellow, green—three colors. That's it. Yet they've successfully guided billions of people for over a century. You don't need more colors. You need better hierarchy."

The Lesson: Your brain doesn't need color to understand importance. It needs visual weight, contrast, and pattern recognition. Once users learn that brown = clickable, they stop thinking and start clicking.

The Three-Button Story

I built them three buttons. Same color. Different stories.

The Hero: Primary Button

Picture this: A user lands on their product page. They're comparing compostable food containers—trying to decide between three options. Amid the product images, descriptions, and pricing, one button catches their eye immediately. It's solid brown with white text. Bold. Unmissable. It says "Add to Cart."

That's the primary button. The hero of the page.

I remember a conversation with Sarah, one of our test users. After completing a purchase, I asked her: "Why did you click that button first?" She laughed. "It was the only one that looked like a button. Everything else looked like... options?"

Exactly.

The primary button doesn't ask permission. It makes a statement. But here's the catch: you can only have one hero per scene. Use two primary buttons on a page, and suddenly neither one feels primary. Use three, and users freeze—paralyzed by choice, unsure which action you actually want them to take.

During EcoPackage's redesign, we identified the moment-of-truth actions:

  • "Add to Cart" on product pages
  • "Submit Order" in checkout
  • "Start Your Eco Journey" on the homepage

These weren't just important actions. They were the actions—the reason the page existed. Everything else? Supporting cast.

What makes it work:

When you hover over it, it darkens to #6f5938—rich, deeper, more intentional. Your cursor becomes a hand. The button almost seems to rise slightly off the page. Every micro-interaction whispers: "Yes, this is clickable. Yes, this is important. Yes, you should click me."

When disabled, it fades to 40% opacity. No ambiguity. No clicking a button wondering why nothing happens. It's honest design.

The Sidekick: Secondary Button

Now imagine the same user, moments before they clicked "Add to Cart." They noticed another button: "View Catalog." It had a brown outline, no fill. Clean. Professional. Definitely clickable—but not screaming for attention.

That's the secondary button. The reliable sidekick.

Here's why it works: the outline creates a clear action area—this is a button, not just text—without competing with the primary action. When you hover over it, the background fills with brown. For a split second, it becomes the hero. Then you move your cursor away, and it recedes back to its supporting role.

It's like a good supporting character in a movie. Always there when you need them, never stealing the spotlight.

We used secondary buttons for:

  • "Learn More" next to "Order Samples"
  • "View Details" next to "Add to Cart"
  • "Browse Products" below the hero CTA

During user testing, one participant said something that stuck with me: "I clicked 'Add to Cart' without thinking. But I stared at 'View Details' for a second—like, do I need more info? Then I decided I didn't. But I liked that it was there."

That pause? That's intentional. Secondary actions should require a moment of thought. They're for users who need more information, want to explore alternatives, or aren't quite ready to commit.

"The best designs don't force decisions. They guide them. A primary button says 'Do this.' A secondary button says 'Or consider this.'"

The Ghost: Tertiary Button

Here's where it gets interesting. What about the actions that shouldn't feel like actions?

"Skip this step." "Maybe later." "Cancel." "Subscribe to Newsletter."

These are the ghost buttons. They're there, but barely. Just underlined text in brown. No border. No background. Minimal visual weight.

I'll never forget watching a user navigate the checkout flow. They filled out shipping info, clicked "Continue to Payment" (primary), then noticed "Subscribe to Newsletter" at the bottom. They ignored it. Didn't even pause. Later, I asked: "Did you see the newsletter option?" They had to think for a second. "Oh yeah... I think so? But I was focused on finishing my order."

Perfect. That's exactly how a tertiary button should behave.

When you hover over it, a subtle brown background appears—just enough feedback to confirm it's clickable. But it never demands attention. It's there for users who want it, invisible to users who don't.

We used tertiary buttons for:

  • "Cancel" in forms (you want users to finish, not quit)
  • "Learn More" links in paragraphs
  • "Skip" options in onboarding
  • Newsletter subscriptions (nice to have, not critical)

The underline is clever. It's familiar—we've been trained since the early days of the web that underlined text is clickable. But it's also unobtrusive. It doesn't grab attention; it earns it.

Size Matters (But Not How You Think)

Three sizes. Large, medium, small. Sounds simple, right?

Wrong.

During the EcoPackage project, I initially designed buttons based on aesthetics. The large button looked impressive—56 pixels tall, generous padding, perfect for the hero section. The small button was cute—just 32 pixels, ideal for tight spaces. The medium? Right in the middle.

Then we ran mobile tests.

A participant tried to click the small "Remove" button next to a cart item. She missed. Tried again. Missed. Her finger covered half the button. "This is annoying," she muttered.

We had made a fundamental mistake: we designed for mouse users, not for human fingers.

The Touch Target Rule

Apple's Human Interface Guidelines recommend a minimum touch target of 44x44 pixels. Google suggests 48x48. Why? Because the average adult fingertip is about 10mm wide. On a high-density display, that translates to roughly 40-50 pixels.

Our small button? 36 pixels. It looked great on desktop with a precision mouse cursor. On mobile, it was a usability nightmare.

We adjusted the sizes slightly:

  • Large (60px height) — The attention grabber
  • Medium (48px height) — The reliable workhorse
  • Small (36px height) — Desktop-only or with caution

Large (60px) — The Hero

This is your homepage CTA. Your "Order Free Samples" button. Your "Complete Purchase."

The large size isn't just about visibility—it's about confidence. A big button says: "This action is important. This is where you should be heading."

But use it sparingly. One large primary button per screen, maximum. More than that, and you're shouting at your users.

Medium (48px) — The Workhorse

This is your default. Most buttons on most pages should be medium.

It's large enough to be easily tappable (comfortably above the 44-pixel threshold), but not so large that it dominates the interface. It scales well across devices. It works in forms, cards, modals, navigation menus.

One designer on the team asked: "Can we use medium for everything?" Honestly? You could. If you had to choose just one size, medium would be the safe bet.

Small (36px) — Use with Caution

Small buttons look clean. They fit nicely in dense interfaces—data tables, admin dashboards, inline actions.

But they're borderline too small for touch devices.

Mobile Consideration: On mobile, prioritize Medium and Large sizes. Small buttons can frustrate users with touch-based input—aim for at least 44px minimum touch targets.

Putting It All Together: Button Hierarchies in Action

Theory is easy. Application is hard.

Let me show you three scenarios where the three-button system either succeeds brilliantly or fails spectacularly.

Scenario 1: The Product Page

A user lands on a product page for compostable takeout containers. They see product images, descriptions, pricing. At the bottom of the page, three buttons:

  • "Add to Cart" — Large, filled, brown background
  • "View Catalog" — Medium, outlined, brown border
  • "Learn More" — Small, underlined, brown text

What happens? Most users click "Add to Cart." Some click "View Catalog" to browse alternatives. A few click "Learn More" to read about materials and certifications.

Why it works: The hierarchy matches user intent. Most people land on a product page ready to buy. Some want to explore. Few want deep technical info. The buttons reflect that reality.

Scenario 2: The Modal Dialog

A user tries to leave the checkout flow. A modal appears: "Are you sure you want to leave? Your cart will be saved."

Two buttons:

  • "Continue Shopping" — Primary, filled
  • "Complete Purchase" — Secondary, outlined

During testing, 60% of users clicked "Continue Shopping" and left. We had accidentally made the exit path the primary action.

We swapped them:

  • "Complete Purchase" — Primary, filled
  • "Continue Shopping" — Tertiary, underlined

The conversion rate jumped 15%.

The lesson: Your visual hierarchy should reinforce your business goal. If you want users to complete checkout, make "Complete Purchase" the hero. Make "Leave" the ghost.

Scenario 3: The Form Footer

A long form for wholesale inquiries. At the bottom:

  • "Submit Request" — Large, filled, full-width
  • "Save Draft" — Medium, outlined, positioned left
  • "Cancel" — Small, underlined, positioned right

One user said during testing: "I love that I can save my draft. I'm not ready to submit, but I don't want to lose my work."

Why it works: The primary action is submit—that's the full-width hero. But secondary and tertiary actions acknowledge user needs: sometimes you need to step away, sometimes you need to bail entirely. The design doesn't punish those choices; it accommodates them gracefully.

Pattern Library:

  • Product Cards: "Add to Cart" (primary) + "Details" (tertiary underlined)
  • Hero Sections: "Start Your Eco Journey" (large primary) + "Browse Products" (outlined secondary) + "Subscribe to Newsletter" (tertiary)
  • Navigation: All medium secondary outlined buttons for consistency
  • Danger Actions: "Delete Account" styled as primary but in red, with "Cancel" as outlined secondary

The Color Story: Why Brown?

"Why not green? You're an eco brand. Green makes sense, right?"

That's what the client asked. And they were right—green would make sense. Forest green was their primary brand color. It appeared in the logo, the header, the accent elements.

But green buttons felt... predictable. Expected. Forgettable.

We chose earthy brown (#8b6f47) instead. Here's why:

1. Differentiation. Every eco brand uses green buttons. Brown made them distinct.

2. Consistency. By reserving brown exclusively for interactive elements, users quickly learned: brown = clickable. They didn't need to think twice. They saw brown, they knew it was a button.

3. Brand Alignment. The brown felt organic, natural, grounded—like composted soil. It reinforced the brand's sustainable values without being heavy-handed.

4. Accessibility. White text on brown (#8b6f47) achieved a 4.5:1 contrast ratio—passing WCAG AA standards. Brown text on white backgrounds was equally readable.

The hover state (#6f5938) deepened the brown, like rich earth after rain. It felt intentional, not just a default color shift.

Accessibility: Everyone Deserves Good Buttons

Six months after launch, I got an email from a user named Marcus.

"I'm legally blind," he wrote. "I use a screen reader. Your buttons actually work. Thank you."

That email made my year.

Accessibility isn't a checkbox. It's not something you bolt on at the end. It's foundational. And buttons—those tiny rectangles we click hundreds of times a day—are often the first place accessibility fails.

Contrast: Can You See It?

White text on brown (#8b6f47) achieves a 4.5:1 contrast ratio. That passes WCAG AA standards. For large text (18px+), it even passes AAA.

Brown text on white background? Same deal. Sufficient contrast. Readable for users with low vision, color blindness, or just bad lighting (like trying to use your phone in bright sunlight).

During testing, we had a participant with deuteranopia (red-green color blindness). I asked: "Can you tell which button is primary?" He pointed immediately to the filled button. "That one. It's darker."

It worked. Not because of color alone, but because of visual weight. The filled background created contrast that transcended color.

Focus States: Can You Navigate It?

Not everyone uses a mouse. Some users navigate entirely by keyboard—tabbing from button to button, hitting Enter to click.

Every button in the EcoPackage system includes a focus ring. It's not subtle. When you tab to a button, a clear outline appears. You know exactly where you are.

We tested this with a user who has motor control issues. She couldn't use a mouse accurately, so she tabbed through the page. "The focus ring is obvious," she said. "I never lose my place."

This isn't optional. It's a legal requirement in many jurisdictions (ADA, Section 508, European Accessibility Act). But more importantly, it's the right thing to do.

Disabled States: Can You Understand It?

When a button is disabled, it fades to 40% opacity. You can still see it. You can still read the label. But it's unmistakably unavailable.

We pair this with proper ARIA attributes:

<button class="btn btn-primary" disabled aria-disabled="true">
  Submit Order
</button>

Screen readers announce: "Submit Order, button, disabled."

No ambiguity. No clicking a button and wondering why nothing happens. It's honest, transparent design.

Screen Readers: Can You Hear It?

A screen reader user doesn't see your beautiful brown buttons. They hear: "Add to Cart, button."

That's it. The label. The role. The state.

So your button labels need to be descriptive. "Click Here" tells a screen reader user nothing. "Download Free Packaging Guide" tells them exactly what happens when they click.

During testing, a screen reader user listened to the product page. She heard: "Add to Cart, button. View Catalog, button. Learn More, link."

Notice the difference? Buttons perform actions (adding to cart). Links navigate (going to another page). The semantic HTML conveys intent.

Mistakes I Made (So You Don't Have To)

Let me tell you about the time I broke the checkout flow.

Mistake #1: Too Many Heroes

Early in the EcoPackage project, I designed a product comparison page. Three products. Three "Add to Cart" buttons. All primary. All large. All filled with brown.

During testing, a user stared at the screen for fifteen seconds. Then she closed the tab.

I asked her why. "Too many choices," she said. "I couldn't figure out which one you wanted me to pick. So I picked none."

That's called decision paralysis. When everything is important, nothing is.

We fixed it: Made one product "Featured" with a large primary button. The other two got medium secondary buttons. Conversions went up 22%.

The lesson: One hero per screen. Maybe two if they're spatially separated. Never three.

Mistake #2: Inconsistent Styling

On the checkout page, I used a filled button for "Cancel Order." It was red, not brown, but still—filled meant primary in our system.

Users kept accidentally canceling their orders. They'd reach for "Complete Purchase" and click "Cancel" instead. Both buttons looked equally important.

We changed "Cancel" to a tertiary underlined button. Tucked it into the corner. Made it small. Suddenly, accidental cancellations dropped to near zero.

The lesson: Establish a visual language and stick to it. Filled = primary. Always. Even for destructive actions, consider using a secondary outlined button in red rather than breaking the pattern.

Mistake #3: Mobile Tap Targets

I already told you about the small button disaster. But here's what I didn't tell you: I ignored the warnings.

A designer on the team said: "That's only 32 pixels. Too small for mobile."

I said: "It looks fine. Users will adapt."

They didn't. They got frustrated and left.

The lesson: Design for fingers, not cursors. 44px minimum for touch targets. No exceptions.

Mistake #4: Vague Button Labels

Our newsletter signup originally said "Submit." That's it. Just "Submit."

One user hesitated. "Submit what? My email? My soul?"

We changed it to "Subscribe to Newsletter." Signups increased 18%.

The lesson: Tell users exactly what happens when they click. "Download Free Guide" beats "Download." "Add to Cart" beats "Submit." "Start Free Trial" beats "Sign Up."

Mistake #5: Ignoring Hover States

In the first version, buttons didn't change on hover. They just... sat there.

A user moved their cursor over "Add to Cart" and paused. "Is this a button?" they asked.

We added hover states: darker background, subtle scale, cursor change to pointer. Suddenly, buttons felt alive. Interactive. Clickable.

The lesson: Users expect feedback. If your button doesn't respond to hover, it feels broken—even if it works perfectly.

How to Build This (Without Breaking Your Site)

Alright, you're sold. You want a clean three-button system. How do you actually implement it?

1. Start with Components, Not One-Offs

Don't write custom CSS for every button. You'll end up with inconsistencies, bloated stylesheets, and a maintenance nightmare.

Instead, create reusable button classes:

<button class="btn btn-primary btn-lg">Order Samples</button>
<button class="btn btn-secondary btn-md">View Catalog</button>
<button class="btn btn-tertiary btn-sm">Learn More</button>

Three button styles. Three sizes. Mix and match. That's it.

If you're using React or Vue, make it a component:

<Button variant="primary" size="large">Order Samples</Button>
<Button variant="secondary" size="medium">View Catalog</Button>
<Button variant="tertiary" size="small">Learn More</Button>

2. Use Design Tokens (Your Future Self Will Thank You)

Hard-coding colors is a mistake. What happens when the brand changes from brown to navy? You'll be searching through hundreds of files.

Use CSS variables:

:root {
  --button-primary-color: #8b6f47;
  --button-hover-color: #6f5938;
  --button-border-radius: 9999px;
  --button-transition: all 0.2s ease;
  --button-disabled-opacity: 0.4;
}

.btn-primary {
  background: var(--button-primary-color);
  border-radius: var(--button-border-radius);
  transition: var(--button-transition);
}

.btn-primary:hover {
  background: var(--button-hover-color);
}

Now when the brand updates, you change one value. Done.

3. Document Your System

A year from now, a new designer joins the team. They see three button styles. They don't know when to use which.

Write it down. Create a style guide:

  • Primary (filled): Use for the single most important action. Limit to one per screen.
  • Secondary (outlined): Use for supporting actions that complement the primary. Multiple per screen OK.
  • Tertiary (underlined): Use for optional, dismissive, or low-priority actions.

Share it with your team. Update it when patterns evolve. Make it a living document, not a PDF buried in Confluence.

4. Test on Real Devices

Your Macbook Pro has a high-DPI display and a precision trackpad. Your users have cracked Android phones with greasy fingerprints.

Test on real devices. Check tap targets on mobile. Verify contrast in bright sunlight. Navigate with a keyboard. Use a screen reader.

You'll find issues. Fix them before launch.

The $15,000 Lesson

Remember that client who spent $15,000 on a redesign? The one with beautiful imagery and terrible buttons?

Six months after we rebuilt their button system, their e-commerce conversion rate had increased 28%.

Same products. Same prices. Same marketing budget.

Different buttons.

Here's what changed:

  • One clear primary action per page (instead of five competing CTAs)
  • Consistent visual language across all 47 pages
  • Proper mobile touch targets (no more missed taps)
  • Accessible focus states (keyboard navigation actually worked)
  • Descriptive button labels (users knew what they were clicking)

Buttons aren't sexy. They don't win design awards. But they drive behavior. They guide decisions. They convert visitors into customers.

Get them right, and your interface feels effortless. Get them wrong, and nothing else matters.

The Takeaway

Great button design isn't about making things pretty. It's about making things work.

Three button styles. Clear hierarchy. Consistent application. Accessible for everyone. That's the formula. It's not complicated. But it requires discipline, testing, and a willingness to prioritize usability over aesthetics.

Now go look at your website. Count the primary buttons on your homepage. If it's more than two, you've got work to do.
