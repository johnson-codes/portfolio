Power Distance and DEI: How Authority and Inclusion Shape Communication

How leadership, hierarchy, and inclusion differ between China and Canada — and what that means for everyday communication

The Office Door That Stayed Closed

One of the earliest impressions I carried from my years growing up and working in parts of China was not dramatic or hostile — it was subtle. The office door often stayed closed. Not locked, just symbolically distant. You didn’t knock unless you already had permission. You didn’t casually bring up complaints with a senior. Authority wasn’t only higher in rank — it felt elevated, almost untouchable.

In family settings, this dynamic showed up clearly too. Elders spoke; younger members listened. Opinions flowed downward but seldom upward. Respect was structural — unspoken, unquestioned, and often unquestionable. The intention was harmony, but the effect sometimes meant avoiding direct communication about issues until someone with authority decided to address them.

The Canadian Shift: Authority That Listens

Moving to Vancouver gradually reshaped how I saw power in action. It wasn’t just about titles shifting; it was about how people with power communicate. I remember meeting a senior manager who welcomed questions before the meeting even began. Eye contact was direct, the handshake firm, and feedback was genuinely sought. Instead of closing doors, leaders here often opened conversations.

In Canada, powerful people often lead with humility and clarity — starting by asking open-ended questions, actively listening, and creating space for voices at all levels. That style aligns with broader societal movements around diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), where leadership accessibility and equitable participation are not just ideals but practiced values in many workplaces. DEI efforts in Canada aim to break down barriers and foster environments where individuals from all backgrounds feel they can meaningfully participate and thrive, regardless of position or identity.

This doesn’t mean power disappears or that hierarchy vanishes — it’s about how that hierarchy functions. A senior colleague here might still make the final decision, but the path toward that decision often involves hearing diverse perspectives and addressing potential barriers to participation.

Family and Workplace Mirrors

At home in China, authority — whether from parents or managers — was more positional. Decisions flowed down, and questions flowed up very carefully, if at all. Bringing up a challenge directly could feel like criticism, potentially threatening harmony or respect. In some cases, communication remained indirect until someone with power translated or mediated the issue.

In Canadian contexts, I saw a more dialogic rhythm even in workplaces. Meetings begin with context, invite input, and explicitly ask if anyone has concerns or needs support. Leaders ask not only “What’s the problem?” but also “How can we solve this together?” This practice aligns with DEI principles that aim to create inclusive spaces where voices at every level are valued and heard.

When Power Distance Meets Cultural Expectation

The challenge arises when these two communication styles intersect. Someone from a high power-distance background may read direct questions as confrontational, while someone from a low power-distance environment may interpret indirectness as evasiveness. Misunderstandings don’t necessarily stem from disrespect — they stem from mismatched expectations about how authority should behave and how feedback should be shared.

In my early Canadian workplace experiences, I sometimes assumed frank feedback meant disagreement or discomfort. Later I learned it was often an invitation to clarify and improve. What felt bold or even rude at first was often simply transparent and collaborative communication.

DEI: Formalizing Inclusive Leadership

In Canadian workplaces, DEI isn’t just rhetoric — it’s often embedded into organizational frameworks. Employers promote diversity, equity, and inclusion not just for legal compliance but to build stronger, more innovative cultures where people feel safe to speak up and contribute. Policies may include leadership training on inclusive behaviors, regular forums for feedback, and efforts to ensure representation at all levels.

This cultural shift doesn’t guarantee perfection — Canada still works on representation and equity across workplaces and institutions — but the intentional focus on inclusion creates a baseline expectation: voices matter, and leaders should listen.

What Stays With Me

Growing up where authority often felt distant taught me patience and respect for structure. Living in Canada, where authority often feels approachable, taught me that respect and dialogue can coexist without hierarchy collapsing.

What I’ve come to appreciate is that communication in any cultural context reflects deeper values about power, trust, and human dignity. In Canada, DEI principles help translate good intent into everyday behavior — making authority less about distance and more about shared understanding.

In the end, effective communication isn’t about erasing hierarchy. It’s about making space for voices at all levels so that problems surface earlier, solutions emerge collaboratively, and people feel seen as contributors rather than observers. That shift — from authority as a barrier to authority as a bridge — has quietly transformed how I approach conflict, collaboration, and conversation.
