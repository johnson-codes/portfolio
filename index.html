My Design Philosophy
The Foundation

Every designer has a philosophy, but mine didn't come from textbooks — it came from watching people struggle. Over time, I realized that good design doesn't begin with colors or grids. It begins with people. Their needs. Their frustrations. Their moments of hesitation that tell you something isn't working.

When Design Fails Users

I've seen what happens when design gets it wrong. A visitor spends ten minutes clicking around, unsure what to do next because every button screams for attention. Labels fade into the background, checkout pages feel like they belong to a different website, and the experience quickly turns from curiosity to frustration.

When that happens, users don't just leave the site — they leave with an impression: this brand is confusing, cheap, and not worth my time. That's the cost of ignoring design principles.

Guiding Frameworks

That's why I rely on frameworks — not as rules, but as anchors.

CARP (Contrast, Repetition, Alignment, Proximity) brings order to chaos.

ERBU (Emphasis, Rhythm, Balance, Unity) makes layouts feel like a journey, not a puzzle.

Gestalt psychology helps me respect how users see patterns, not just parts.

And Hick's Law reminds me that less is almost always more.

It's like when a parent says, "Choose A or B." The choice is easy. But if they say, "Choose from A to Z and rank your top ten favorites," suddenly you freeze. Users feel the same way online. Too many choices create paralysis. Fewer choices create clarity — and clarity drives action.

Design with Intention

For me, following these principles means I'm not throwing pixels onto a screen and hoping they stick. Every element has intent. Navigation feels natural. A checkout page feels like part of the same story as the landing page. The design disappears, and what's left is trust, flow, and ease.

The Heart of Design

At its core, my philosophy is simple: design should feel effortless. When users stop noticing the interface and simply do what they came to do — buy, learn, explore, connect — that's when design succeeds. That's when people remember the experience, not the friction.

Information Architecture
Project Overview

When I first launched my own website — consulting.johnsonmarketer.com — I didn't give much thought to information architecture. I focused on visuals and features, but overlooked how information was structured and how users would actually navigate the site. The result was predictable: high bounce rates, no orders, and frustrated visitors.

After usability testing, I realized the problem wasn't the content itself but the way it was organized. Users couldn't find what they needed, and the site failed to guide them toward conversion. That feedback became the turning point, pushing me to rethink and rebuild the site's IA with a user-centered approach.

The Challenge

Usability testing revealed several critical gaps in the site's information architecture:

No search function, making it difficult to find services
Lack of secondary navigation, leaving visitors stuck at top-level menus
No related or bundled suggestions to guide exploration or cross-sell
Missing filter options, forcing users to scan through long lists manually
No breadcrumb trail, so users often felt lost on the site
My Solution

I restructured the site's IA through user-centered research and testing. A key method was card sorting, which revealed how users naturally grouped categories. This ensured the navigation reflected user mental models, not just internal assumptions.

Key improvements included:

Global Navigation A top-level menu for clear orientation
Secondary Navigation Sub-menus drilling into categories
Breadcrumb Trail Showing location and enabling quick jumps back
Filter/Facet Functions Refine results by attributes like price
Search Functions Predictive search with autocomplete, category tabs, etc
Cross-Linking / Related Items Suggestions such as "You may also like"
Collapsible Menus Progressive disclosure to keep hierarchies manageable
Logical Grouping Categories organized using card sorting
